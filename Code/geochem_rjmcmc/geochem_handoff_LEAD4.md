## Why the section changed (one paragraph to brief co-authors)
The LEAD_3 geochemistry treated 153 EPMA and 50 LA-ICP-MS analyses as independent samples. In fact they come from 7 and 2 tektites. LEAD_3 also included EPMA analyses with totals down to 33 wt%, quantified three LA-ICP-MS sites with a wrong internal standard, and reported "low" Pd that was actually 177 ppb, an analytical artefact. Its "rj-MCMC" was a 15-combination model selection, and no code existed for it. LEAD_4 re-screens the data, re-implements the reversible-jump unmixing of Carter et al. (2025, G³; the Berkeley Geochronology Center model of Fuentes, Renne and co-authors) with the regional compilation of Sieh et al. (2019) as the source dictionary, and restricts the claims to what the data support and what agrees with the regional literature.

### Methods → Geochemical Analysis (replaces both paragraphs)

Major elements of Australasian tektites were measured by electron probe microanalysis (EPMA; 153 spot analyses on seven tektites: MNG24_A3_1–3, MNG24_B1, MNG24_C2 and MG24_B1 from three localities near Muong Nong, Savannakhet Province, Laos (~16.5°N, 106.5°E), and KK24_T1 from Khon Kaen, northeastern Thailand) [instrument, accelerating voltage, beam current, beam diameter and standards to be supplied by P. Sanchez]. Because tektite glass is essentially anhydrous, analyses with oxide totals below 97 wt% were rejected as compromised by vesicles, alteration or polishing relief, following the screening criterion of Carter et al. (2025). This retains 82 analyses on four tektites (MNG24_A3_1, n = 30; MNG24_B1, n = 17; MG24_B1, n = 5; KK24_T1, n = 30). Every analysis of MNG24_A3_2 (totals 33–86 wt%), MNG24_A3_3 (61–97 wt%) and MNG24_C2 (66–94 wt%) was rejected, as were four marginal analyses (96.3–96.9 wt%) of MNG24_B1 and MG24_B1 (Fig. 14a). Accepted analyses were recomputed anhydrous with total Fe as FeO. Trace elements were measured by laser-ablation ICP-MS on two tektites from sample MNG24_A3 (MNG24_A3_1 and MNG24_A3_2; five ablation sites each, five replicate analyses per site) using EPMA SiO2 as the internal standard [instrument, calibration and secondary reference materials to be supplied]. Three MNG24_A3_2 sites had been standardised to SiO2 values (27–37 wt%) taken from rejected EPMA points; because Al/Si, which is independent of the internal standard, is identical in the two tektites, all MNG24_A3_2 analyses were re-standardised to the accepted MNG24_A3_1 mean (75.0 wt% SiO2). Relative to EPMA on the same tektite, LA-ICP-MS returns Al2O3, MgO and TiO2 30, 7 and 8 % high and MnO 38 % low, and most trace-element concentrations lie 3–38 % above the median of published Indochinite and Muong Nong analyses (Cr is 18 % below it; Ni is twice it, though within the published range) (Fig. 16a). Absolute LA-ICP-MS concentrations are therefore treated as accurate to no better than ±30 %, and interpretation relies on inter-element ratios (Th/Sc, Eu/Eu*, rare-earth pattern shape) that are insensitive to the internal standard and to most calibration offsets. Candidate target lithologies sampled in this study – Khorat Group sandstone (n = 10) and mudstone (n = 3), lateritic saprolite developed on basalt (n = 4), the tektite-hosting diamicton or “catastro-loess” (n = 9) and Bolaven basalt (n = 2) – were analysed for major elements by X-ray fluorescence and for trace elements by ICP-MS on pressed-powder pellets. They were combined with the regional compilation of Sieh et al. (2019), which includes analyses from Sanematsu et al. (2011) and Saminpanya et al. (2014) among other sources: Bolaven basalt (n = 252 after removing 62 analyses with LOI > 3 wt% or totals outside 98.5–101.5 wt%), Khorat Group sandstone (49) and mudstone (5), laterite and saprolite on basalt (47), sedimentary laterite and bauxite (37), Bolaven loess (13; grouped here with the diamicton) and felsic metatuff (8; dacitic–rhyolitic, Na2O 2.6–5.7 and K2O 3.0–5.5 wt%, major elements only). Three further samples labelled metatuff in the compilation were excluded: two are altered mafic rocks (SiO2 43–57 wt%, LOI 8–9 wt%) and one is Na-leached (Na2O 0.01 wt%). The field context of the metatuff (unit, and whether it lies beneath the Khorat Group) is not documented in the compilation. Values reported below detection were set to half the detection limit. The basalts were divided at the alkaline–subalkaline boundary of Macdonald & Katsura (1964) into tholeiitic basalt and basaltic andesite (n = 229) and alkali basalt and basanite (n = 25, including both basalts analysed here), because the two suites differ several-fold in incompatible-element contents (median Th 1.6 versus 7.0 µg/g; Th/Sc 0.07 versus 0.46). Published Indochinite and Muong Nong-type tektite analyses (n = 262; e.g. Koeberl, 1992; Amare & Koeberl, 2006) provide the regional reference.

Tektite source lithologies were inferred with the reversible-jump Bayesian unmixing model of Carter et al. (2025), re-implemented here (code archived with the data). Each EPMA analysis is modelled as a mixture, Y = C·M, of K components whose compositions (C) and per-analysis mixing weights (M) are unknown; K itself is a free parameter (Gallagher et al., 2009). Following Carter et al. (2025), each component is drawn from a dictionary of lithology types, each type is assigned a truncated-normal prior on every oxide, the prior on K is Poisson with mean 10 (truncated at 30), the mixing weights of each analysis carry a Dirichlet prior, and the likelihood is Gaussian on centred-log-ratio-transformed data and model. The dictionary comprises the eight regional lithology types listed above (prior medians and robust standard deviations (1.4826 × MAD) from our analyses and the regional compilation of Sieh et al. (2019), with standard deviations of at least 15 % of the median, or 25 % for types with fewer than five analyses), an unweathered post-Archean shale (PAAS; Taylor & McLennan, 1985; ±20 %) to represent fine-grained sediment not affected by tropical weathering, and an unconstrained “random” component. Nine oxides were used (SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3, FeOt, MnO, MgO, CaO, Na2O, K2O); Cl, SO3 and Cr2O3 are at or below detection. Where Carter et al. (2025) used a uniform 0.5 % relative uncertainty, we combined per-oxide EPMA accuracy (1–5 %, 20 % for MnO) in quadrature with a 5 % mixing-model error, because tektite melting does not conserve every oxide exactly. Posterior sampling used Metropolis–Hastings with within-model moves (composition, weights, component-type swap) and between-model moves (birth, death, split and merge), step sizes adapted to an acceptance rate of 0.234 during burn-in. As in Carter et al. (2025), twelve chains were started from random states with K drawn uniformly from 2–30 and run for 6 × 10⁴ iterations, the first half being discarded as burn-in. Once a chain has converged, between-model moves are almost never accepted, because adding or removing a component perturbs the fit to all 82 analyses at once; the posterior on K and on component identity is therefore represented by the spread among chains. Chains whose median log-likelihood lay more than 50 units below that of the best chain (i.e. trapped in a much poorer mode) would have been excluded; none were. Uncertainties combine within- and between-chain variance (Carter et al., 2025). Sensitivity runs (four chains each, 3 × 10⁴ iterations) excluded the random component, excluded Na2O and K2O, and restricted the dictionary to the regional lithologies (no PAAS or random component). Trace elements were not inverted. Instead, the bulk posterior mixtures were combined with the median trace-element contents of each lithology to predict Th/Sc and Eu/Eu*, which were then compared with the LA-ICP-MS data.

### Results → Geochemistry: Testing the Bolaven Source Hypothesis (replaces the whole subsection)

The impact hypothesis makes a testable geochemical prediction: if the Australasian tektites are melts of the Bolaven target column, their composition should be reproducible as a mixture of the local Mesozoic (Khorat Group) sediments and Bolaven basalts (Sieh et al., 2019) – a claim contested on geochemical grounds (Mizera, 2022). Previous work on Indochinite and Muong Nong-type tektites established that their parent was a fine-grained, quartz-rich sediment with a Jurassic-age crustal Sr–Nd signature and a near-surface ¹⁰Be inventory, possibly with a minor mafic contribution (Blum et al., 1992; Koeberl, 1992; Ma et al., 2004; Amare & Koeberl, 2006). We ask what our new data add to this, restricting claims to what the data can support: (i) which analyses are reliable, (ii) which of the regional lithologies a Bayesian unmixing model requires, (iii) whether trace-element ratios agree, and (iv) whether a projectile signature is resolvable.

**Data quality.** Only 82 of the 153 EPMA analyses, on four tektites, have oxide totals of at least 97 wt% (Fig. 14a). The rejected analyses (totals 33–97 wt%, including every analysis of MNG24_A3_2, MNG24_A3_3 and MNG24_C2) are not interpreted. The accepted analyses lie within the field of published Indochinite and Muong Nong tektites on every Harker diagram (Fig. 14b–h); tektite means span 70.8–80.6 wt% SiO2, with CaO 1.0–2.4, Na2O 1.2–1.6 and K2O 2.4–2.8 wt% (anhydrous), the most siliceous being KK24_T1 from Thailand. The LA-ICP-MS data come from only two tektites from one sample (MNG24_A3), and their absolute concentrations carry a calibration offset of up to ~30 % (Methods; Fig. 16a); they characterise two specimens, not the strewn field. Their inter-element ratios nonetheless reproduce the regional tektite signature: Th/Sc = 1.14 and 1.20, Eu/Eu* = 0.69 and 0.66 and La/Th = 2.8 and 2.5, against literature medians of 1.33, 0.62 and 2.8.

**Bayesian unmixing.** All twelve chains reproduce the 82 accepted analyses equally well (median log-likelihoods -265 to -220; interquartile per-analysis residuals within ±3 % for every oxide except MnO; Fig. 15d), but they do so with between 6 and 25 components, so the number of components is not constrained by the data (Fig. 15a,b). What is reproducible among chains is the type of material required (Fig. 15c). (1) The deeply weathered lithologies that now cover the plateau – lateritic saprolite on basalt, sedimentary laterite and bauxite, and the tektite-hosting diamicton or “loess” – are not required (combined weight 1.9 %, present in at most 17 % of posterior samples). (2) A quartz-rich sand classified as Khorat sandstone (36 ± 15 %) is required in 91 % of samples. (3) The remainder must be a fine-grained aluminous component carrying ~1.5–2 wt% each of CaO and Na2O. No sampled Khorat sediment supplies this, because all are intensely leached (median CaO ≤ 0.04 and Na2O ≤ 0.15 wt%; Fig. 14e,f), so the model assigns it to the unweathered-shale (27 ± 23 %) and felsic metatuff (10 ± 6 %) types, whose posterior compositions move toward the tektite composition itself. These two types should be read as proxies for unleached, Na–K-bearing fine-grained material, not as identified source rocks: PAAS is a global average, and the field context of the regional metatuff is not documented. (4) A Ca–Mg-rich component is permitted but not uniquely identified: Bolaven basalt contributes 4.8 ± 5.4 % (tholeiite 3.7 %, alkali basalt 1.1 %; present in 64 % of samples) – in this run the alkali basalts, despite their higher K2O and Na2O, are scarcely used – and an unconstrained “random” component of intermediate composition takes 18 ± 13 %, which itself indicates that the dictionary does not contain an adequate parent. The basalt and random components trade off: without the random component, basalt rises to 19 ± 9 % (tholeiite 10 %, alkali basalt 9 %) and becomes required. Otherwise the sensitivity runs give the same picture (without the random component: sandstone 26 %, unweathered shale plus metatuff 22 %, basalt 19 ± 9 %, weathered regolith 1.0 %; with Na2O and K2O excluded: sandstone 40 %, unweathered shale plus metatuff 34 %, basalt 7 ± 9 %, weathered regolith 2.4 %; with only the regional lithologies (no PAAS or random component): sandstone 25 %, unweathered shale plus metatuff 31 %, basalt 18 ± 2 %, weathered regolith 0.7 %; Fig. 15e). Restricted to the regional lithologies, the model substitutes felsic metatuff (31 ± 13 %), the only sampled regional rock that is not leached of Na and K. Lateritic saprolite is never used. The unmixing therefore supports a sediment-dominated parent, in agreement with earlier work, but shows that the tektites cannot be melts of the Khorat sediments and regolith as they are exposed today. A less-weathered equivalent – fresh Khorat mudstone at depth, or the unweathered sediment that preceded the Pleistocene laterite – would be required, and no such material has yet been analysed.

**Trace elements.** On the chondrite-normalised REE diagram the two tektites match the published Indochinite pattern: light-REE-enriched with a negative Eu anomaly, at concentrations within or slightly above (≤ 20 %) the literature range (Fig. 16b). In the calibration-insensitive ratios (Fig. 16c) they fall among the regional sediments and unweathered shale (Th/Sc 0.8–2.0, Eu/Eu* 0.58–0.70) and away from the Bolaven basalts (Eu/Eu* ≈ 1.0–1.1; Th/Sc ≈ 0.07 for the tholeiites and 0.46 for the alkali basalts) and basaltic saprolite. Mixed with even the most Th-rich regional sediment (Khorat sandstone, Th/Sc ≈ 2.0), about 10 % tholeiite or 20 % alkali basalt lowers Th/Sc to the tektite value (≈ 1.2), and ~20 % alkali basalt raises Eu/Eu* above the tektite range, so the trace ratios allow at most ~10–20 % basalt-derived material, depending on the suite. The felsic metatuff has no trace-element analyses and is omitted from the following prediction. The bulk posterior mixtures predict Eu/Eu* = 0.59–0.71 (90 % range), consistent with the tektites, and Th/Sc = 0.73–1.42, which brackets the observed values but is mostly lower (median 0.95). They also under-predict absolute Zr and Th (predicted 151 and 9 µg/g, against literature medians of 299 and 14.5 µg/g), because the regional sandstones are poor in zircon (median Zr ≈ 110 µg/g). The trace-element budget therefore does not close with the existing endmember data, mirroring the major-element result: the parent sediment was less weathered, and probably richer in heavy minerals, than the Khorat rocks sampled at the surface.

**Platinum-group elements.** In 34 of the 50 LA-ICP-MS analyses both ¹⁹¹Ir and ¹⁹³Ir are zero, and the non-zero values (0.3–4.4 ppb) do not agree between the two isotopes (Fig. 17b). Ir is therefore below detection, with an upper limit of about 4 ppb (~1 % of CI chondrite). The reported Pd (0.02–0.38 µg/g; median 0.18 µg/g) cannot be real. It would be ~350 times the upper-crustal abundance (0.52 ppb; Rudnick & Gao, 2003) and ~30 % of CI chondrite (McDonough & Sun, 1995), while Ir (< 1 % CI) and Ru (≈ 0.3 ppb) are at or below crustal levels – a Pd/Ir and Pd/Ru fractionation unlike any terrestrial or meteoritic reservoir. Instead, the site-mean Pd/Y ratio is nearly constant (0.006–0.012; Fig. 17a). On ¹⁰⁵Pd, the usual analysis mass, this corresponds to an ⁸⁹Y¹⁶O⁺/⁸⁹Y⁺ production rate of 0.13–0.26 %, a typical value for LA-ICP-MS, so the reported Pd is most plausibly a yttrium-oxide interference. The isotope monitored was not recorded and should be confirmed. Pd is not interpreted further. Laser ablation cannot test for a projectile at the level expected here: the non-radiogenic Os of Laotian Muong Nong and splash-form tektites indicates at most ~0.005 % of a chondritic component (Ackerman et al., 2019), which would add ~0.02 ppb Ir – two orders of magnitude below our detection limit. Ni, Co and Cr in MNG24_A3_1 (78, 18 and 62 µg/g) lie within the published Indochinite ranges (10th–90th percentiles: Ni 21–181, Co 11–25, Cr 58–184 µg/g). Because the Bolaven basalt and its laterites are themselves rich in these elements (median Ni 160 and 210 µg/g), the siderophile elements do not discriminate between a projectile and a mafic or lateritic target contribution. Cu (10–14 µg/g) and Zn (48–52 µg/g) are lower than in average upper crust (28 and 67 µg/g), as is commonly reported for tektites, but given the calibration uncertainty and the unknown composition of the true parent we draw no inference from them.

**Summary.** Within the limits of the data – four tektites with reliable major elements and two with trace elements – the geochemistry establishes four points, each consistent with the regional literature. First, the tektites are sediment-dominated melts of a quartz-rich sand plus a fine-grained aluminous sediment. Second, that parent was much less weathered than any Khorat sediment or regolith now exposed on the Bolaven Plateau, which cannot supply the tektites’ Ca and Na. Third, a basaltic contribution of about 5–20 % is permitted; it is required only when the model is denied an unconstrained component, and the trace-element ratios cap it at ~10 % (tholeiite) to ~20 % (alkali basalt). Fourth, LA-ICP-MS cannot detect a projectile component at the ≤ 0.005 % level indicated by Os isotopes. None of these results discriminates the Bolaven location from any other Indochinese source with a similar sedimentary cover; a decisive test would need analyses of unweathered Khorat sediment from depth and isotopic (Sr–Nd, ¹⁰Be) comparison with the tektites.
